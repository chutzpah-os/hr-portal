I already told the story of how this method came together, and the research behind each piece of it, in [the main article on the Language Acquisition Formula](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula). This one skips all of that. This is just the twelve weeks, stage by stage, with what an actual day inside each one looks like.

One honest thing first: the twelve weeks are a path, not a promise. How fast you move through them depends on the language, how close it is to the one you already speak, how much time you give it each day, and how consistently you show up. What you get from following the stages in order isn't a guaranteed date. It's always knowing exactly what to do that day, and why — which turns out to be the part that actually keeps people going.

## Stage 0 — Day 1: Planning

This stage is exactly one day, and you don't learn a single word of the language in it. You write down why you actually want this language, not the polished version, the honest one. You turn "become fluent" into something you can watch yourself do, like holding a ten-minute conversation about your day. You pick a fixed time slot, at least 30 minutes, and attach it to something you already do every day — right after breakfast, right before bed. And you record Video Zero: one to five minutes of yourself trying to speak the language, however badly. You don't watch it again until week 12.

That's the whole day. No vocabulary, no stories, no apps. Just decisions made once, on a good day, so you don't have to make them again on a bad one.

## Stage 1 — Week 1: Alphabet and sounds

This is the stage people want to skip, because it doesn't feel like "real progress" yet. On a Tuesday in week 1, you're not opening a vocabulary list. You're listening to a single letter's sound, over and over, then repeating it out loud, then writing it by hand a few times, then inventing a slightly absurd mental association so it sticks. If the alphabet is the same as yours, this takes a few hours spread across the week. If it isn't, it takes longer, and that's time well spent — every word you learn from here on passes through how you read it and how you hear it, and a wrong sound locked in now gets expensive to unlearn later.

## Stage 2 — Weeks 1–2: The 200 words

You split 200 of the most common words in the language — not animals or furniture, the words that actually hold a sentence together — into blocks of roughly 28 or 29, one block a day for about a week. For each word, you skip the translation and draw a small, slightly ridiculous image instead, link it to something from your own life, put it in a sentence. At the end of the day, you review that block and the previous ones with flashcards, and the very last thing you do before sleep is a short pass over everything so far. By the end of the week, covering the translations, you can remember most of them without looking.

## Stage 3 — Week 2: Seven stories

You get seven short stories, built almost entirely out of the 200 words you just locked in, each one a situation you'll actually run into — ordering a coffee, asking for directions. You don't move to story two until you've worked story one through all seven steps: general idea, context, full understanding, listening with your eyes closed picturing it, speaking along with the audio, speaking along without it, and going back to whatever part kept tripping you up. By the seventh day, you listen to all seven back to back, and the first one — which felt hard on day one — now sounds simple.

## Stage 4 — Weeks 3–8: Creating need

This is the longest stage, and it's where your own life becomes the study material. Say it's a Tuesday in week 5. You need to tell a coworker you'll be a few minutes late. Before you touch a translator, you try to build that sentence yourself, out loud or in your head, with what you already know. If you can't finish it, you look it up, write it on the right-hand side of a notebook split in half — your language on the left, the new one on the right — and say it in the moment. Next time you need that exact sentence, you try to remember it before opening the notebook again. On the side, you're watching light videos on subjects you actually like, aiming for the general idea, not every word.

## Stage 5 — Weeks 9–12: Losing the fear

The videos get a little harder. The notebook sentences get built in your head before the translator becomes the backup instead of the first move. And two new things start: Solo Immersion, talking to yourself out loud for 10 to 30 minutes a day about whatever happened in your day, and at least one real conversation a week, with an actual person — 10 minutes the first week, longer as the weeks go on. You'll freeze mid-sentence at some point in week 9 or 10. That's not a setback, that's the exact moment the fear starts losing its grip, because nothing bad happens when you freeze and keep going. At the end of week 12, you record the second video and only then do you watch Video Zero again, side by side with it.

## Stage 6 — Ongoing: Autonomy

The roadmap ends at week 12. The language doesn't. From here it runs in four-week cycles: look at where you're still getting stuck, plan around it, review weekly, adjust. If progress stalls — a plateau, which happens to almost everyone — the fix is usually switching to native-speaker material and widening your vocabulary past the original 200, not studying harder at the same level. Grammar comes in now too, but only to fix a mistake you're actually making, one rule at a time, not as a subject on its own.

## FAQ

#### What if twelve weeks isn't enough for me?
Then you keep going past week 12 — nothing about the method breaks if it takes you fourteen weeks or twenty. The stages move by checkpoint, a concrete task you can actually observe yourself completing, not by the calendar. Someone with an hour a day will move through this differently than someone with thirty minutes, and both finish with the same thing: a language they can use.

#### Can I skip a stage if I already know some of the language?
Yes, but skip based on the checkpoint, not a guess. If you can already read the 200-word list out loud without stumbling, you've effectively passed Stage 2 — test yourself against the actual checkpoint before moving on, instead of assuming.

#### Do I need to finish all seven stories before I ever watch anything in the language for fun?
No — nothing stops you from enjoying the language outside the method. The stage order is about what your study time is built around, not a ban on everything else. Just don't be surprised if native content feels like noise before Stage 3 is done; that's expected, not a sign you're behind.

Twelve weeks from now, the point isn't a certificate or a score. It's one actual conversation that simply wouldn't have happened without those twelve weeks — and multiplying that one conversation, many times over, is the entire premise of the [Problem Solver Foundation](https://problemsolverfoundation.vercel.app/), the organization I run alongside writing these methods. Its goal is a billion people connected by 2035, not as a slogan, but because health, education, energy, security and economic stability don't get fixed by one person working alone, and right now an uncomfortable number of the people who could be working on them together simply can't talk to each other yet. Finishing this roadmap is one small, personal proof that the gap is closeable.

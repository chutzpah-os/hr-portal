I found the first person I ever had a real conversation with in English on an old forum, on a free video platform that doesn't really exist anymore. A guy from the United States. A girl from Russia. Neither of them knew me, owed me anything, or had any reason to be patient with how bad my English actually was.

I was not ready. I want to be very clear about that, because the advice to "wait until you're ready" is probably the single most expensive piece of advice in language learning, and almost everyone gets it at some point, usually from someone who means well.

Readiness doesn't arrive by studying more. It arrives from having had the conversation already — which is a problem, because you can't have had it until you have it.

## Why "when I'm ready" never actually arrives

If you've been putting off your first conversation, you're not lazy and you're not uniquely afraid. You're responding correctly to a feeling that's lying to you. The feeling says: once I know enough, this won't be uncomfortable. The truth is closer to the opposite — the discomfort is what makes you ready, not something you wait out before starting.

Every week you postpone it, the postponement itself becomes evidence that the conversation must be a bigger deal than it is. That's how a ten-minute phone call turns into a thing you've been "about to do" for eight months.

So this isn't really an article about grammar or vocabulary. It's about logistics — how to actually get a real person on the other end of a conversation this week, what to say in the first thirty seconds, and what to do the moment you inevitably freeze.

## Step 1: find a person, not a tool

An app that talks back to you is useful for almost everything in this method except this one specific job. A conversation partner needs to be unpredictable in the way only a real person is — asking a follow-up you didn't expect, laughing at the wrong moment, getting distracted and changing the subject. That unpredictability is exactly what you're training for, and nothing scripted replicates it.

A few places that actually work:

- Language exchange apps, where you trade your language for theirs — this was mine, a forum pointing to a free video chat site, and it's only gotten easier to find since.
- Online or in-person conversation clubs built specifically for this.
- Community groups of speakers of the language where you live, which exist in almost every city bigger than a small town.
- Interest-based social media groups about a topic you already care about, where the language is just what people happen to be speaking.
- One paid lesson with a teacher, used only for conversation — tell them explicitly you don't want grammar, you want to talk.

Pick one. Not all five. Picking all five is just a more elaborate way of postponing.

## Step 2: say this in the first thirty seconds

Almost everyone becomes more patient the moment they know you're learning. Say it early, plainly: *I'm learning this language, so please be patient with me.* It costs you nothing and buys you an enormous amount of goodwill — the other person adjusts their speed, picks easier words without being asked, and stops expecting fluency they were never going to get anyway.

Before the call, write down two or three topics you can already talk about, even badly. Where you're from, what you do, why you're learning this language. You don't need more than that. The conversation will go wherever it goes — you just need a door to walk through it with.

## Step 3: keep a rescue phrase within reach

You will get stuck. Decide right now what you'll say when it happens, so you're not inventing a plan in the panic of the moment:

- Could you repeat that, please?
- Could you speak a little slower?
- How do you say this?
- Sorry, I don't quite understand yet.

Saying one of these calmly is the actual skill here — more than any word you'll learn that week. It converts a freeze into a normal exchange instead of a dead end.

## What actually happened on my first call

I mixed up words. I asked them to repeat things more than once. I had long pauses where I was clearly searching for something and not finding it. None of that stopped the conversation. We kept going, because that's what real people do when both sides are trying — they work around the gaps instead of ending the call over them.

What I remember from that night isn't the mistakes. It's the moment I understood that I had actually communicated something to someone from a completely different part of the world, in a language that, a year earlier, I couldn't use at all. That's the entire reason this matters more than any grammar exercise ever could — it's the first proof that the thing you've been studying is actually usable.

If the idea of this still makes your stomach drop, that's normal, and it's worth reading [how to work through that fear specifically](/en/blog/how-to-lose-the-fear-of-speaking-a-new-language) before you schedule the call — Solo Immersion especially will make the first minutes less jarring.

## Setting it up this week

Pick the platform today. Message someone or book the slot today, even if the conversation itself is three days out — the scheduling is the part that actually breaks the avoidance loop, more than the conversation itself. Write your two or three topics and your rescue phrases tonight. Then show up, freeze a little, and let it be imperfect.

This entire approach — building toward a real conversation deliberately instead of hoping readiness shows up on its own — is Stage 5 of [*The Language Acquisition Formula*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HQXZPVZM), and it only works because of what comes before it: enough vocabulary, enough listening, enough of your own sentences already written down in a Mirror Method notebook that you're not starting from zero when the call connects.

Here's a scenario I think about a lot: two researchers on opposite sides of the planet, independently working on pieces of the same unsolved problem, neither one aware the other exists, because nothing ever puts them in the same room — least of all a shared language. That's close to the actual reason I run the [Problem Solver Foundation](https://problemsolverfoundation.vercel.app/): the belief that big problems, in health, education, energy, security and economic stability, get solved by people finding each other, not by one person working alone, with the goal of a billion people connected across professions and borders by 2035. A real conversation in a new language is a small, concrete version of that connection actually happening instead of almost happening.

## FAQ

#### Where can I find someone to practice a real conversation with?
Language exchange apps, online conversation clubs, community groups for speakers of the language in your city, interest-based social media groups, or a single 30-minute lesson with a teacher used only for conversation, never grammar. The one requirement is that the person is real and actually responds to you.

#### Is it okay to use a translator during the conversation?
Yes, especially the first few times — having it nearby takes some of the pressure off. The goal over time is to build the sentence in your head first and only check the translator, instead of starting with it.

A guy I was coaching messaged me eight weeks before a work trip to Mexico City. "I don't need to acquire Spanish," he said. "I need to survive five days of meetings and not look like an idiot at dinner."

That's a completely different brief than "I want to speak Spanish." And it changes everything about how you spend the time you have.

Most advice about learning a language for travel assumes you have months, or it assumes you just want to order a coffee and say thank you. A business trip sits in an awkward middle: you need more than tourist phrases, you don't have time for the full twelve-week version of [the Language Acquisition Formula](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula), and the stakes — a client, a colleague, a deal — are higher than "did I enjoy my vacation."

Here's how I'd actually spend six to eight weeks if that were my brief.

## Decide what "ready" means before you start

The first mistake is studying as if the goal were fluency, then feeling like a failure in week six because you're nowhere near fluent. You were never going to be. Fluency isn't on the table in eight weeks, and pretending it is just wastes the hours you do have.

What's actually on the table: understanding most of a conversation that's about your work, producing a handful of solid sentences about yourself and your project without freezing, getting through a hotel check-in, a taxi, a restaurant and small talk at dinner without needing your colleague to translate for you. That's a real, achievable target. Write it down as a sentence — "I want to introduce myself, talk about my role, and get through dinner without going silent" — the same way Stage 0 of the method asks you to write a goal as an action instead of a vague feeling.

## The 200 words still come first — just faster

I don't change the order of the method for a short timeline. I compress it. The ~200 most common words in any language still carry most of a basic conversation, and they're still worth the first push, just over days instead of a couple of weeks. Give yourself the first week, split into daily blocks, and treat the image-and-drawing technique from Stage 2 as non-negotiable — a word with a real image sticks after one pass, a word you just stared at doesn't, and you don't have review cycles to spare for words that didn't land the first time.

Skip the stuff that's interesting but not urgent. You don't need the word for "giraffe." You need *necesito*, *gracias*, *dónde está*, *puede repetir*, *lo siento*, *trabajo*, *reunión*. The words that keep any exchange moving, not the words that would be nice to know eventually.

## Build your Mirror Method around the trip itself, not around life in general

Normally the Mirror Method grows out of your actual day — whatever situation comes up, you build the sentence for it. On a trip timeline, you don't have weeks for your life to hand you situations one at a time. So you front-load it: sit down and write out, in your notebook, the sentences from the trip you can already picture.

- Checking into the hotel, asking about the room, asking where something is.
- Getting a taxi or rideshare, giving an address, asking how long it'll take.
- Ordering at a restaurant, asking what something is, saying you have a dietary restriction.
- Introducing yourself at the meeting — your name, your role, your company, why you're there.
- Talking about your own project in two or three sentences you can say without thinking.
- The handful of rescue phrases that get you out of any hole: *could you repeat that*, *could you speak more slowly*, *I'm still learning, thank you for your patience*.

Write the native-language version on one side, the target-language version on the other, exactly like the Mirror Method table in the main method. Say every one of them out loud, more than once, before you leave. The goal isn't to memorize a script — it's for your mouth to have already made these specific sounds enough times that they come out under a little pressure.

## Use an AI assistant to rehearse, then rehearse with a real person

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that actually matters. Stage 6 of the method, which normally handles someone with a specific professional goal, points at exactly this sequence: rehearse with an AI voice assistant first, playing the role of a colleague or a waiter or a hotel clerk, until the sentences stop feeling foreign in your mouth. Then — and this part isn't optional — rehearse the same scenarios with an actual person. A tutor for one session, a friend who speaks the language, anyone real who'll push back, mishear you, ask a follow-up you didn't expect.

An AI assistant is patient and infinitely repeatable, which is exactly what you need for the first ten reps of a sentence. It's also predictable in a way a real meeting never is. The real person is what tells you whether the sentence survives contact with an actual human being who might answer something other than what you rehearsed for.

## Accept what you're not going to have

You will not be negotiating in the language. You will not be catching a joke that lands on a cultural reference. You will probably not be smooth. What you will have, if you spend six to eight weeks on this instead of zero, is the difference between a trip where you need a translator for every sentence and one where you can greet people properly, follow the shape of a conversation, hold your own for a few exchanges, and lean on help only when it actually matters. That gap is bigger than it sounds, and it's the one that's realistic to close before your flight.

If the trip goes well and you decide you want more than survival-level Spanish, French or whatever you were up against, that's when the full twelve-week version of the method is worth running properly — I lay the whole thing out, stage by stage, in [*The Language Acquisition Formula*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HQXZPVZM). But for the trip itself: the 200 words, a notebook full of sentences you actually need, and a couple of rehearsals with a real person will get you further than you think.

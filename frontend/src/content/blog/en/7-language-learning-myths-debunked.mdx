Some advice about learning a language gets repeated so often that nobody checks it anymore. It sounds like common sense, it gets passed from teacher to student to friend, and eventually it hardens into something people treat as fact — right up until you actually go look at the research behind it, and there isn't any.

I went looking, because every one of these cost me time I didn't need to lose while I was putting together the [Language Acquisition Formula](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula). Here are seven of the most common ones, and what actually holds up.

1. **"Everyone has a learning style — visual, auditory or kinesthetic — and should study accordingly."** This one sounds almost scientific, which is probably why it spread so far. Reviews of the research looking specifically at whether matching teaching to someone's preferred style improves results came back empty — no solid evidence that it does (Pashler et al., 2008). People do have preferences. What actually helps isn't sorting yourself into one lane and staying there; it's combining images, sound and real use, which happens to be closer to how everyone's memory actually works, preference or not.

2. **"You can learn a language by listening to audio while you sleep."** Sleep is genuinely important here — people who go to sleep a few hours after studying new vocabulary remember more of it than people who stay up much longer first (Gais, Lucas and Born, 2006). That's sleep *consolidating* what you studied while awake. Learning brand-new vocabulary from scratch during deep sleep is a different claim entirely, and it isn't supported. Play the audio while you're awake and paying attention, even partial attention during a commute. Save sleep for what it's actually good at: locking in what you already put in.

3. **"Just expose yourself to the language and it'll come on its own."** Exposure matters — nobody acquires a language from zero exposure. But exposure to something you don't understand is just noise, and plenty of people spend years with a language playing in the background of their lives, understanding almost none of it, and never move past that. The input has to be *comprehensible* — material you follow well enough that the gaps fill in from context — or the hours barely count.

4. **"You only really learn by living abroad."** Living somewhere boosts exposure and need, and both genuinely help. But the place itself isn't doing the work — plenty of people live abroad for decades while working, socializing and watching everything in their own language, and plenty of people have never left home and speak beautifully because they engineered exposure and need into an ordinary week. A plane ticket was never the mechanism. The two ingredients underneath it are, and you can build both without one.

5. **"Some people are just born with a gift for languages, and some people aren't."** Natural aptitude exists and it does vary — some people pick up patterns a little faster than others, same as with music or sports. What it mostly changes is speed, barely anything about the ceiling. Every person with normal hearing has already acquired at least one language, often two or three as a kid, without anyone screening for talent first. The people who conclude they "don't have the gift" are almost always describing a bad method, not a diagnosis.

6. **"Starting with grammar is the logical way to learn a language."** It feels logical because that's how school taught you to add before you'd add — rule first, then application. Language doesn't actually run on that order. One of the most influential distinctions in this field splits conscious *learning* — knowledge about a language — from natural *acquisition*, the kind that lets a sentence come out without being built on purpose first (Krashen, 1982). Grammar studied up front produces the first kind, which is too slow to use mid-conversation. It still has a real job later, fine-tuning mistakes you're already making out loud — just not as the entry point.

7. **"A good app is basically enough on its own."** Apps are genuinely strong at vocabulary and daily contact, and I'm not telling anyone to delete theirs. What they can't simulate is a person who interrupts you, asks something you didn't rehearse for, and waits — out loud, in real time — for your answer. That's where almost everyone actually freezes, and it's the one piece of acquisition that needs another human on the other end, no matter how good the app gets.

Notice the pattern running through all seven: the myth usually isn't a flat lie, it's a half-truth stretched past where the evidence actually goes. Sleep helps — just not the part people think. Exposure matters — just not without understanding. Talent is real — just not as decisive as people assume. Knowing exactly where each one breaks is what lets you stop wasting time on the part that doesn't work and put it into the part that does.

## About the author

I'm Haniel Rolemberg, and I believed more than one of these myths myself before I had any reason to question them. Clearing up myths about how to actually acquire one is, to me, just the first step toward making that call connect more often.

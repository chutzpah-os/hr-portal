People assume the Language Acquisition Formula is just something I pieced together from what worked for me and for the polyglots I interviewed years ago. That part is true. What I didn't do was stop there. Once the method had a shape, I went back and checked whether any of it had real research behind it, instead of just trusting that it had worked for a handful of people I knew.

It had. More than I expected, honestly.

## Most of what you forget, you forget fast

In the late 1800s, a psychologist named Hermann Ebbinghaus ran an unglamorous experiment on himself. He memorized lists of meaningless syllables and tracked how much he could still recall after a few hours, then a few days. The pattern he found has been replicated since, including in a more recent study by Murre and Dros in 2015: most of what you lose, you lose early — in the first hours and the first day. After that, the forgetting slows down.

That one curve is the whole argument for spaced repetition. If most of the damage happens fast, reviewing a word while you still remember it well is close to a waste of time, and reviewing it after you've already lost it means relearning it from zero. The useful moment is right before you'd forget — that's when a review does the most work, and it's why the method has you review the 200-word foundation right up against the edge of forgetting, not on a fixed schedule that ignores it.

## Trying to remember beats reading it again

Here's a trap almost everyone falls into: rereading a list of words feels like studying, because everything looks familiar by the third pass. That feeling is misleading you. Recognizing a word is a different, much easier mental operation than pulling it out of memory on your own.

Roediger and Karpicke ran a study in 2006 where some students reread a text several times and others tested themselves on it without looking. A week later, the group that had tested themselves remembered far more. The act of forcing your brain to retrieve something, and sometimes failing, is what makes the memory stronger — not the exposure.

That's why, in the method, you don't reread your vocabulary list. You cover the translation and try. Flashcards work for exactly this reason. Plain rereading doesn't, no matter how many times you do it.

## A word with a picture has two ways back

If you store a word only as text, it has one path back into memory: text. Link it to an image instead, and now it has two — the sound and spelling on one side, the picture on the other. This is called dual coding, a principle from research by Paivio in 1971, and it's not a new idea dressed up in new language. Back in the 1600s, an educator named Comenius was already putting a drawing next to every word in his language books, because he'd noticed, without any lab to prove it, that children remembered a word better when it came with a picture.

This is the actual reason the method has you draw a small, often ridiculous image for each of the first 200 words instead of just writing the translation next to it. The weirder the image, the more it stands out from the other 199, and the easier it is to pull back out.

## Sleep isn't a bonus — it's part of the process

A study by Gais, Lucas and Born in 2006 looked at people learning vocabulary in a new language. Those who went to sleep a few hours after studying remembered more of it than those who stayed awake much longer before going to bed. Sleep isn't just rest after the work is done. It's when part of the consolidation actually happens.

That's the entire reason the method puts a short review right before bed. It's not a ritual for the sake of discipline. It's timed on purpose.

## Fear doesn't just make you freeze — it blocks input from getting in at all

This is the one that matters most to me personally. Stephen Krashen, back in 1982, described something he called the affective filter — the idea that anxiety doesn't only hurt you when you're trying to speak. It also reduces how much of what you're listening to or reading actually gets absorbed in the first place. You can be looking straight at a sentence, understanding it on paper, and still not retain it, because part of your attention is busy bracing for judgment.

I spent years avoiding conversations because I wanted every sentence perfect before I opened my mouth. What this research says is that the fear itself was costing me input, not just output. That's why the method treats a mistake as proof you tried, not as something to apologize for.

## The other half of Krashen's work: understanding almost all of it beats understanding none of it

The affective filter is only one piece of what Krashen proposed. The other piece, and probably the single most useful idea in this whole list, is comprehensible input — material you can understand almost completely, with just one small unfamiliar part. Too hard, and your brain files it as noise. Too easy, and there's nothing left to acquire from it. This is the entire reason the method doesn't throw you into native-speaker podcasts on day one. It starts with short, controlled stories first, and I walk through exactly how and why in [a separate article on comprehensible input](/en/blog/comprehensible-input-explained) — it's the idea most people get backwards first, usually by jumping into "real" content because it feels like faster progress, when it's mostly just noise at that stage.

## One thing science does not back up

Since I'm citing the research that supports the method, I should also say what doesn't hold up, because it gets repeated constantly in language-learning spaces. The idea that everyone has a fixed "learning style" — visual, auditory, kinesthetic — and should only study that way has been reviewed seriously, including by Pashler and colleagues in 2008, and the evidence for matching instruction to a preferred style simply isn't there. What actually helps is combining image, sound and use — which, conveniently, is exactly what dual coding and the method both already ask you to do, regardless of which "type" of learner you think you are.

None of this is about proving I was right for the sake of being right. If I'm going to ask you to put in 30 minutes a day for 12 weeks, I'd rather you know the daily tasks are built on something sturdier than a hunch — mine or anyone else's.

If you want to see how all of this fits together into one system instead of five separate findings, the full formula — M + SR + CI + AO — is laid out in [the main article on the method](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula), and in much more detail in the book itself, [*The Language Acquisition Formula*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HQXZPVZM).

Daniel had been reading Spanish documentation at work for years. He understood almost everything on a page. He also kept closing the tab on job postings that paid well, did exactly what he already did, and ended with one line: "interview conducted in Spanish." He did that for two years before anything changed.

If that's close to your situation — real passive knowledge, a real wall the moment speaking is required in a specific, high-stakes setting — this article is about building the narrowest possible bridge across that specific wall, not a general fluency plan.

## Why "just get fluent" is the wrong plan here

General fluency is the goal of [the full twelve-week method](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula), and it's the right target if your timeline is open-ended. An interview is not open-ended. It has a date. Trying to build broad fluency in three weeks before a deadline usually produces someone who's a little better at everything and still not reliably good at the one twenty-minute conversation that actually matters.

The better move, once you have some real foundation already — some vocabulary, some listening, some ability to hold a basic exchange — is to narrow hard. Stage 6 of the method covers exactly this kind of adaptation, building a specialized track for one specific goal instead of spreading effort evenly across everything a language could eventually be used for.

## Build a Mirror Method notebook just for the interview

You already know, roughly, what an interview is going to ask you. Use that predictability. Open a dedicated section of your notebook — or a new one entirely — and fill it with the sentences you'll actually need, not general vocabulary:

- How you'd introduce yourself and your background in under a minute.
- How you'd describe one project you're proud of, specifically — what you built, what problem it solved, what you learned.
- How you'd answer "why do you want this role" and "why this company" in your own real words, not a template.
- How you'd ask a clarifying question if you don't understand something, calmly, without it reading as a lack of confidence.
- How you'd talk about a mistake or a failure, honestly, which almost every interview eventually asks about in some form.

Write the native-language version first, then the translation, the same way the Mirror Method normally works — except instead of waiting for your day to hand you the sentence, you're deciding in advance exactly which sentences you need, because you already know the shape of the conversation that's coming.

## A short list of words for your field, not the language in general

Alongside the 200 general words the broader method starts with, build a second, much shorter list — somewhere around 100 words — specific to your industry and role. The technical terms that come up constantly in your actual work, the ones a generic course would never prioritize because they're useless outside your field and essential inside it.

Use the same technique as the rest of the method: an image for each word, not just a flat translation, and a sentence from your own actual work that uses it. This list pays for itself fast, because interview conversations stay inside a narrower topic range than everyday life does — you're far more likely to need "deployment" or "quarterly revenue" in an interview than you are to need the word for "cutlery."

## Rehearse with AI first, then with a person — in that order, not instead of

An AI assistant that can hold a voice conversation is a genuinely good first rehearsal partner for this specific use case, for one reason: it removes the social stakes while you're still clumsy. Ask it to play the interviewer, give it your field and the role, and run through your prepared questions until the sentences stop feeling like translations in your head and start feeling like things you just say.

Then do the exact same rehearsal with a real person — a friend, a tutor, someone from a language exchange, even someone unrelated to your field. The gap between "an AI that mostly follows my lead" and "a person who reacts in a way I didn't plan for" is exactly the gap between rehearsal and the real thing, and skipping the second step is the single most common way this kind of prep quietly fails.

## Be honest about the timeline you actually have

If you have months: build the general foundation first — vocabulary, listening, some real conversations — and layer the interview-specific track on top near the end. This is the strongest version of this plan, and it's the one Daniel eventually used, after two years of closing tabs, to finally sit through and pass an interview conducted entirely in Spanish.

If you have a few weeks: skip straight to the narrow track. You won't come out broadly fluent, and that's fine — the goal is a focused twenty-minute conversation about your own experience, not a general command of the language. Prepare your sentences, drill your field's vocabulary, rehearse with AI and then a real person, and accept that anything outside the interview's likely topics will still feel shaky. That's a realistic outcome, not a failed one.

If you have days: focus entirely on the introduction, the project story, and two or three rescue phrases — *could you repeat that, please? Could you rephrase the question?* A short, well-rehearsed answer to the three questions you're almost guaranteed to get beats a scattered attempt at everything.

## It will still feel uncomfortable, and that's fine

Even with all of this prepared, you'll likely freeze somewhere in the actual interview. That's not a sign the prep failed — it's what [facing a real, higher-stakes conversation](/en/blog/language-anxiety-why-you-freeze-up) actually feels like the first few times, regardless of how ready you are on paper. The preparation doesn't remove the discomfort. It gives you something solid to fall back on in the middle of it.

I put the full version of this — the broader foundation this narrow track sits on top of — in [*The Language Acquisition Formula*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HQXZPVZM), because an interview is usually not where someone's need for a language starts. It's where it becomes impossible to keep ignoring.

The clearest version of this I know is the one in this article itself: a hiring manager who already knows exactly the kind of person they need, and a candidate who is exactly that person, kept apart by nothing except an interview neither of them asked to also be a language test. I run the [Problem Solver Foundation](https://problemsolverfoundation.vercel.app/) on the belief that the big problems — health, education, energy, security, economic stability — get solved by people finding each other, not by one person working alone, and the whole point is connecting a billion people across professions and borders by 2035. Every interview someone gets through despite the language is one fewer version of that story that ends with both sides losing.

## FAQ

#### How far in advance should I start preparing for an interview in another language?
Ideally, months, with some everyday foundation already in place before you narrow in on interview-specific prep. With only a few weeks, narrow prep alone can still get you through a focused 20-minute conversation about your own experience, even without broad fluency — it just won't generalize well beyond that topic.

#### Can I really prepare for an interview using only AI practice, with no native speaker?
You can get a long way, and it's a good first rehearsal partner precisely because it removes the social stakes. But the checkpoint that actually matters is a real person, because real reactions, interruptions and follow-up questions are exactly what you can't fully simulate, and exactly what an interviewer will do to you.

Someone mentioned "LAF" to you, or you found it searching for a way out of years of classes that went nowhere, and now you want a straight answer before you spend a single minute on it. Fair. Here's the straight answer.

## What it actually is

LAF stands for the Language Acquisition Formula — a method I built out of my own path learning languages, years of mentoring people through theirs, and research on memory and second-language acquisition. It isn't a school, an app, or a course you log into. It's a twelve-week roadmap, split into six stages, that tells you exactly what to do each day and why, so that a language stops being something you study and starts being something you use.

The whole thing compresses into one line: **Acquisition = M + SR + CI + AO** — memorization, spaced repetition, comprehensible input, and active output, with planning underneath all four. I break down what each letter actually means to do, day by day, in [a separate piece on the formula itself](/en/blog/m-sr-ci-ao-explained).

## Who it's actually for

It's for someone who can give a language 30 minutes a day, every day, for at least three months, and who's tired of studying grammar that never turns into a sentence when a real person is waiting for an answer. It works whether you've never touched the language or you've already got years of classes behind you and nothing to show for it in conversation — the roadmap just starts you at a different stage.

It is not for someone looking for ten minutes a week, or for someone who wants a guarantee of a specific result by a specific date. I'm straight about this in the book, and I'll be straight about it here: the twelve weeks are a path, not a promise. How fast you get through it depends on the language you pick, how close it is to your own, how much time you actually give it, and how consistent you are. What I can promise is that you'll know exactly what to do on any given day, and why — which is more than most people get from a classroom.

## Why I believe it works

I didn't build this from a theory I liked. I built it from watching what hyperpolyglots — people who speak ten, twenty, even fifty languages — actually did differently, then checking that against the research. A few pieces of that research, by name, because "trust me" isn't good enough on its own:

- Stephen Krashen's distinction between *learning* (conscious knowledge about a language) and *acquiring* (language you can actually use without building it rule by rule) — this is the whole reason grammar-first classes produce people who can describe a verb tense and can't tell you what they did yesterday.
- Hermann Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve, later confirmed by Murre and Dros in 2015 — most of what you study is lost in the first hours and the first day, which is exactly why spaced repetition reviews a word right before you'd lose it, not before or after.
- Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 study on testing versus rereading — trying to recall a word strengthens it far more than reading the translation one more time.

None of that is marketing copy. It's the reason the method is built the way it is.

## Where to actually start

Today, not Monday. Stage 0 takes one day: write down why you want this language, turn that want into a goal you can watch happen ("hold a 10-minute conversation about my day," not "become fluent"), pick a fixed daily time slot, and record a short video of yourself trying to speak the language right now, however badly. You won't watch that video again until week 12. It's the only proof you'll have that you didn't start exactly where you're standing now.

If you want the whole roadmap end to end, [this is where I laid it all out](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula), and the full day-by-day mechanics of all six stages are in [*The Language Acquisition Formula*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HQXZPVZM).

## A few questions people actually ask me

#### Is LAF a course, an app, or a book?
It's a method, written down as a book. There's no app to buy, no subscription, no login. A notebook, your phone, and 30 minutes a day are the only tools it asks for.

#### Does this work for a language completely different from my own, like one with a different alphabet?
Yes, but it takes longer. Stage 1 exists specifically to fix the sounds and symbols before a single word gets memorized, so nothing gets stored with the wrong pronunciation. A language like Japanese or Arabic needs more hours in that stage than one that shares your alphabet, but the order of the stages doesn't change.

#### Is this just comprehensible input with a new name?
Comprehensible input is one of the four letters, not the whole formula. On its own, it builds understanding without building the ability to answer — plenty of people understand a podcast perfectly and still freeze the moment someone asks them a direct question. CI needs memorization underneath it and active output on top of it, or you end up with someone who can listen and can't speak.

#### What if I don't finish in exactly twelve weeks?
Then you take thirteen, or sixteen. The checkpoint at the end of each stage is what tells you you're ready to move on, not the calendar. The twelve weeks are a reasonable average, not a deadline with a penalty attached.

That's the straight answer. If it sounds like something you'd actually do for 30 minutes a day, start with Stage 0 today.

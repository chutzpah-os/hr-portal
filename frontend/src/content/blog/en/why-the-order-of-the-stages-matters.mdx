Every few weeks someone tells me they're going to "do the formula, but faster" — skip the stories, skip the alphabet work if the language shares their own, go straight to conversation because that's the part that actually matters. I understand the impulse completely. I also know, from watching it happen more times than I can count, exactly where it breaks.

The six stages aren't six optional modules you can shuffle. They're built on each other, in this order: Planning, Alphabet and sounds, Vocabulary, Listening and reading, Creating need, Losing the fear, and then an open-ended Autonomy stage after that. Skip ahead and you're not moving faster. You're borrowing time from a stage you haven't finished, and it gets collected later, usually with interest.

## Skipping Stage 1 to get to words faster

This is the one people notice least, because it doesn't feel like skipping anything. You already know the alphabet — it's the same one you use — so why spend hours on sounds?

Here's what actually happens. The letters look the same, but a good number of them don't sound the same. In Spanish, the *h* in *hola* is silent and the *j* in *jamón* is a sound your native alphabet doesn't really have. If you skip straight to memorizing words, you memorize them with your own language's sounds glued onto someone else's letters. The word and the wrong pronunciation go into memory as a single unit, together, at the same time. Unlearning a pronunciation later takes far more repetition than learning it correctly the first time would have — you're not adding a correction, you're fighting a habit that's already settled in.

## Skipping Stage 2 to get to conversation faster

This is the one that hurts the most, because it looks like dedication. "I'll just start talking to people now, I'll pick up words as I go." Real need, real contact — those are two of the four ingredients that make acquisition work, so this instinct isn't wrong. It's just early.

Without the roughly 200 words that carry an ordinary conversation, every single sentence you want to say requires a lookup. There's nothing in memory to retrieve, so there's nothing to say without a translator open in your hand. A few conversations like that, and most people don't conclude "I need more vocabulary." They conclude "I'm bad at this," and they stop. The foundation isn't there yet to make the conversation feel like progress instead of exposure.

## Skipping Stage 3 to get to using the language sooner

Short stories built from your own 200 words can feel like busywork once you've got the words down — you already know them, why read seven stories about ordering coffee and asking for directions?

What the stories actually do is show you how words you already know combine into sentences, in what order, in what rhythm, without a single grammar rule explained. Skip them and move straight into using the language in your day, and you'll find yourself needing a rule for word order that you'd otherwise have absorbed for free, just from hearing the same patterns enough times in a row.

## Studying grammar before you've acquired anything

This is the biggest one, because it's exactly what school trained you to do, and it feels responsible. Shouldn't you understand the rules before you try to use the language?

A rule studied before you have anything in memory to attach it to stays abstract — a sentence about a sentence, with nothing underneath it. It doesn't come with examples you've lived through, so it doesn't come to mind fast enough to use while someone's waiting for your answer. Grammar has a real job in this method. It comes in Stage 6, after you're already speaking, as a tool to fine-tune a mistake you're already making out loud, one rule at a time, always tied to something real you said. Studied early, the same rule is just one more thing to memorize that doesn't yet connect to anything.

## The order is the point

None of the stages are hard on their own. What makes the method work is that each one hands you exactly what the next one needs, in the order your memory actually uses it. Skip one, and the next stage doesn't fail loudly — it just gets quietly slower, because it's trying to build on a foundation that isn't finished yet.

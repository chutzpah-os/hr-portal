For about a week, I had the same last task of the day no matter how tired I was: a few minutes with a stack of flashcards, right before turning off the light. Not scrolling through a list. Not rereading anything. Just the word on one side, my attempt to remember what it meant, then flipping it to check. Some nights it took four minutes. That short, almost boring habit does more work than people give it credit for, and most people who try spaced repetition apps and quit on them never actually got to feel that.

## Why "reviewing" and "rereading" are not the same thing

The forgetting curve was mapped out more than a century ago by Hermann Ebbinghaus, who memorized nonsense syllables and tracked what he still remembered hours and days later. A more recent study by Murre and Dros in 2015 found a very similar pattern. Most of what you lose, you lose fast — in the first hours, then the first day. After that the bleeding slows down.

That curve tells you exactly when a review is worth something. Review a word while you still remember it clearly, and you're wasting a slot — your brain already has it. Review it after you've already forgotten it, and you're not reviewing, you're relearning from nothing. The useful window is the moment just before it would have slipped. Spaced repetition apps exist specifically to find that moment for you and put the word back in front of you right then, with the gap growing a little every time you get it right.

That's also why rereading your vocabulary list over and over feels productive and mostly isn't. A study by Roediger and Karpicke in 2006 had some students reread a text repeatedly and others test themselves on it without looking. A week out, the self-testers remembered significantly more. Recognizing a word you're staring at is easy. Pulling it out of memory with nothing in front of you is the part that actually strengthens it — and that's what a flashcard forces you to do, if you use it right: answer first, flip second.

## Where spaced repetition actually belongs in the method

Here's the part that surprises people who've used flashcard apps for years and still don't feel like they're getting anywhere: in the Language Acquisition Formula, spaced repetition isn't a lifetime habit. It's concentrated almost entirely on the first 200 words, in the vocabulary stage, over roughly a week.

That's deliberate. Those 200 words show up constantly in ordinary conversation in any language — pronouns, basic verbs, connectors — and they're worth drilling hard because everything after them depends on having them automatic. But vocabulary doesn't stop growing after that week. It keeps growing, just not through flashcards. From the stories onward, new words arrive embedded in situations, in your own sentences, in conversations — repeated by your actual life, at intervals your life sets for you, not an app.

If you kept adding every new word you ever encountered into a flashcard deck forever, you'd be running a never-ending review queue that keeps pulling your attention back to studying about the language, instead of using it. That's the exact trap the method is built to avoid. Spaced repetition gets you through the foundation fast. After that, repetition comes from living in the language, not from an app reminding you.

## Why the cards themselves matter as much as the schedule

One more detail most people get wrong: what's actually on the card. The default move is word on one side, translation on the other — plain text both sides. That's weaker than it looks, because two pieces of text sitting next to each other blur together in memory far more easily than a word and an image do.

This is dual coding, a finding from Paivio's research in 1971, and it's old news dressed as new: a 17th-century educator named Comenius was already pairing every word in his teaching books with a drawing, centuries before anyone ran a study to prove he was right. A word stored only as text has one path back into memory. A word linked to a vivid, slightly ridiculous image of your own has two. That's the entire reason the method has you draw something for each of the 200 words instead of just writing the translation next to it — the card isn't just a review tool, it's a second route back to the word.

Spaced repetition isn't broken. Most people are just using it on the wrong material, for the wrong length of time, with the wrong thing written on the card.

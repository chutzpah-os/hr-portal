People ask me what the letters mean before they ask me anything else about the method. M, SR, CI, AO. It looks like an acronym someone cooked up for a slide deck. It isn't. Each letter is a specific job that has to get done, in a specific order, and I only landed on that order after years of watching my own mistakes and other people's.

Here's the whole thing, in one line, the way I wrote it in the book:

**Acquisition = M + SR + CI + AO**

I think of it as a bridge. You're on one side, not speaking the language. You want to get to the other side, where you are. Each letter builds one part of that bridge, and none of them does another one's job.

## M is the planks

Memorization is the first thing you lay down, and it has to be laid down right, or nothing else holds weight. I don't mean memorizing lists of random words. I mean the roughly 200 words that actually carry a normal conversation in any language — not "elephant" and "engineer," but *I need*, *I want*, *because*, *I don't understand*.

Here's what day one of this actually looks like. You pick a word. Say it's *dinero* — money, in Spanish. You don't write "dinero = money" on a line and move on, because that line is forgettable, two pieces of flat text that look like a hundred other lines. Instead you build an image. I use a made-up chain of diners, DinerXYZ, with a giant D on every corner — anyone who owns that many restaurants has a lot of money. You draw it badly in your notebook, you add a sentence, you move to the next word. Twenty, thirty words like that, on day one, and you've laid the first planks.

## SR is the nails

A plank without a nail slides out of place in a week. Spaced repetition is what keeps the words from sliding.

Day one of SR is small and almost boring: you open a flashcard app — any free one — and you put in the words you just memorized. That's it. The app does the one thing that matters, which is show you a word again right before you're about to forget it, not while you still remember it clearly and not after you've already lost it. Each time you get it right, the gap before the next review gets a little longer. You don't need to think about the schedule. You need to show up and answer honestly.

## CI is crossing the bridge slowly, watching where you step

Comprehensible input is language you almost completely understand, with a small new part. This comes a little later in the roadmap — after you've got your first words down — but the mechanics are the same from day one of this stage onward.

What that looks like in practice: a short story, written mostly with the words you already memorized, that you listen to and read until every line makes sense. Not a movie. Not a native podcast at full speed. If you can't follow nine sentences out of ten, the material is doing you less good than it looks like it's doing, because your brain treats it as noise instead of language.

## AO is going back and forth across the bridge

A bridge exists to be walked on in both directions. Active output is you walking it.

On day one of this part, it's not a conversation with a stranger. It's smaller than that — a single sentence from your actual day, said out loud in the moment you needed it. "I need to leave in five minutes." You try to build it yourself first. If you can't, you look it up, write it down, say it. That's the whole unit.

## The part that isn't a letter

Underneath all four is planning, and it doesn't get its own letter because it's not one more thing to do — it's what makes you actually do the other four on the days you don't feel like it. A short weekly check-in: what did I plan, what did I do, what got stuck, what changes next week. And a video of yourself, recorded on day one, saying whatever you can — even nothing — so that twelve weeks from now you have proof of where you started, because you will not feel yourself improving day to day. Nobody does.

Most people who tell me "I tried something like this and it didn't work" were doing one of these four well and ignoring the rest. All memorization and no output gives you a head full of words that never turn into sentences. All input and no output gives you someone who understands a podcast and freezes when a real person asks a question. The formula is a sum for a reason — each part is cheap on its own and expensive to skip.

One more thing, since it's the actual reason I write about any of this instead of just mentoring people one at a time. I run the [Problem Solver Foundation](https://problemsolverfoundation.vercel.app/), built on one belief: we're all problem solvers, just badly connected to each other. The goal is a billion people connected across professions and borders by 2035, because the problems that actually matter — health, education, energy, security, a stable economy — never get solved by one person working alone. A language you haven't acquired yet is one of the most literal walls between the farmer who has a real problem and the engineer who already has the fix for it, or between a teacher and an AI researcher who have everything to say to each other and no shared words to say it in. Every one of the four letters above is in service of taking down that one specific wall.

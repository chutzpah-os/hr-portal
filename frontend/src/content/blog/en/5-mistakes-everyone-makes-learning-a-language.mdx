Most of the people who message me about a language they're stuck on aren't lazy, and they're not missing some talent the rest of us have. They're putting in real hours, every week, and something about how they're spending those hours is quietly working against them. These five mistakes are the ones I see on repeat — in students I've coached, and, more than I'd like to admit, in myself before I built the [Language Acquisition Formula](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula) around not making them anymore.

None of these are beliefs about yourself, and none of them are myths about how languages work in general. They're things you're actually doing with your hands and your time, which means they're also the easiest ones to fix the moment you notice them.

1. **Switching stories or shows the second you get bored, instead of repeating the same one.** The first time through any piece of content, almost all your attention goes to figuring out what's happening. There's nothing left over to notice how the sentences are actually built. Once you already know the plot, your attention frees up to catch word order, verb forms, the phrases that keep repeating — which is exactly how structure settles in without a single grammar lesson. Jumping to something new the moment the first listen feels easy throws away the pass where the real learning happens. Stick with one story or clip far longer than feels natural, until you can follow it without strain, before you move on.

2. **Copying and pasting translations instead of typing them out, word by word.** This one is sneaky because it feels productive — you've got the sentence, it's in your notebook, job done. But the Mirror Method only works because building the sentence by hand forces you to look at every word and notice how it fits together. Copy and paste skips that step entirely, and the sentence passes in front of your eyes without ever really landing. If you're keeping your Mirror Method on your phone, type every word yourself, even when the translator hands you the whole sentence for free.

3. **Studying grammar before you have any real sentences to fine-tune.** It feels responsible to "get the basics right" before you start talking, so people open a grammar book on day one. The trouble is that a rule with nothing to attach to stays abstract — you can know a form exists and still fail to produce it in the half-second a conversation actually gives you. Grammar does real work later, correcting a mistake you're already making out loud, where it has an example from your own mouth to attach itself to. Studied first, with nothing to fix yet, it mostly just delays the point where you start actually using the language.

4. **Running five apps, two podcasts and a textbook at once instead of committing to one main source.** This usually comes from good intentions — more input can't hurt, right? In practice, splitting your attention across a pile of different materials means you never get enough repeated contact with any single one of them to really absorb it. A story you've heard ten times teaches you more than ten different stories you've each heard once. Pick one main source for input and stick with it until it's genuinely too easy, then move up — don't treat variety as the goal.

5. **Skipping the planning and just "starting," with no why, no goal and no fixed time.** This is the mistake that looks the least like a mistake, because it feels like momentum — you're excited, so you just dive in. The problem shows up in week two or three, on the first day motivation dips, when there's no plan to fall back on and every session becomes a fresh decision you have to talk yourself into. People with an actual written reason, a specific goal stated as an action, and a fixed daily time slot don't have to decide anything on a hard day — they just follow what they already decided when they felt motivated. Thirty minutes spent on that setup before you start saves you from the slow fade that quietly ends most people's attempts.

What ties all five together is that none of them look like failure while they're happening. You're still "doing the work" — watching content, filling a notebook, studying grammar, trying four apps, getting started with enthusiasm. The hours just aren't landing the way they could, and the fix in every case is the same kind of fix: less spread thin, more repeated, more deliberate.

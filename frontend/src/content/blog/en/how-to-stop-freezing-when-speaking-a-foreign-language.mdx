In my book, I tell the story of a designer named Lia — a fictional character, but her situation isn't rare at all — who gets asked, mid-interview, to switch to Spanish and talk about a project she's proud of. She understands every word. She knows the words for what she wants to say. And she freezes, manages three seconds of silence, and gets out "Yo... perdón. Mi español... no es bueno." The interview goes back to her own language and ends the way they both already expected.

If something like that has happened to you, I want to say the thing I say in the book first: the problem isn't you. It's a specific, well-documented mechanism, and it has a specific fix.

## What's actually happening when you freeze

Researchers who study this — Horwitz, Horwitz and Cope, back in 1986 — found that the fear of speaking a second language isn't the same thing as general nervousness. It's its own distinct kind of anxiety, tied specifically to the fear of being judged, and it directly damages performance. People who know the answer freeze anyway, and the reason is almost mechanical: when you feel evaluated, part of your attention goes to bracing for that judgment, and there's less left over for the actual job of finding the words and building the sentence.

That's the whole thing. It's not that you don't know the language. It's that fear is competing with the language for the same limited attention, and fear usually wins, because it feels more urgent in the moment than a verb does.

This fear is learned — usually in a classroom, over years of being corrected in front of other people — which is the good news, because what's learned can be unlearned. Not by willing it away. By doing something specific instead.

## The fix isn't "relax." It's two concrete things.

**First: carry a small set of rescue phrases you don't have to build from scratch under pressure.** When your mind blanks mid-sentence, you're not inventing a way to ask for help — you're reaching for something you already have ready. Four that I give people starting out, in the book's example language:

- *¿Puede repetir, por favor?* — Could you repeat that, please?
- *¿Puede hablar más despacio?* — Could you speak more slowly?
- *¿Cómo se dice esto en español?* — How do you say this?
- *Perdón, no entiendo.* — Sorry, I don't understand.

Mentioning early in a conversation that you're learning the language does more work than people expect, too. Almost everyone becomes more patient the moment they know that, and some of the pressure that was aimed at your performance disappears on its own.

**Second: face the fear in doses you can actually survive, instead of either avoiding it completely or jumping straight into the deep end.** Avoidance feels like relief in the moment, and that relief teaches your brain that avoiding was the right call — so the fear comes back a little bigger next time. The opposite also works the opposite way: every time you face a version of the conversation that scares you and nothing bad actually happens, the fear gets a little smaller.

That's why, in Stage 5 of the formula, nobody goes straight from silence to a real conversation with a stranger. First comes Solo Immersion — talking to yourself out loud, in the language, for 10 to 30 minutes a day, about something as ordinary as what you did that morning. It feels strange for about three days. Then it stops feeling strange, and your own voice in the language becomes familiar before a single other person is involved. Only after that does a real conversation enter the picture, and even then, it starts at 10 or 15 minutes with someone patient, not an hour with someone who's in a hurry.

You will freeze during that first conversation. That's not a sign it isn't working. Freezing and continuing anyway is the exact experience that takes the fear's power away — you can't talk yourself out of being afraid, but you can prove to yourself, once, that freezing doesn't end the world, and that proof is worth more than any amount of preparation.

The sentence you're afraid to say isn't missing. It's just competing with fear for your attention, and fear only gets quieter the same way it got loud — through repetition, in doses small enough that you survive every single one of them.

For a while, I made a living writing about people who spoke thirty languages, and I was terrified every time one of them might speak English back to me.

I was a volunteer for a language club, interviewing hyperpolyglots for their blog — people who spoke ten, fifteen, forty languages — and I used the interview as a shield. I asked the questions. They answered. As long as I kept doing that, nobody would notice that my own English fell apart the second a real conversation started.

That's the shape fear takes with a language you're still learning. Not loud panic — just a hundred small avoidances that feel like caution. You wait for the sentence to be perfect in your head before you say it. You let someone else carry the conversation. You tell yourself you'll speak "when you're more ready."

I want to walk you through why that doesn't work, and what does.

## Why avoiding a conversation makes the fear worse, not better

Every time you dodge a conversation, something happens inside your head that feels like relief and works like reinforcement. You skip the call, the chat, the small talk with the barista in the new language, and the tension disappears. Your brain logs that outcome as a win: avoiding was the right move. Next time a similar moment shows up, the fear arrives a little faster and a little stronger, because it was "right" last time too.

Do that for a few years — which is exactly what traditional classes trained most of us to do, by punishing mistakes with grades and laughter — and you end up with adults who can discuss grammar on paper and go silent the second a real person is waiting for an answer.

The reverse is also true, and it's the whole premise behind this part of [*The Language Acquisition Formula*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HQXZPVZM): when you face the situation that scares you and nothing bad happens, your brain registers that instead. Do it again, in a slightly bigger dose, and the fear shrinks a little more. That's not a metaphor. It's the same mechanism behind exposure-based approaches to almost any fear, and speaking a language just happens to be one that nearly everyone carries quietly.

## Training alone before you train in public

There's an old story about Demosthenes, who became one of the great speakers of Athens after starting out with a weak, muddled voice that got him laughed off the stage. Instead of waiting to get good, he's said to have shut himself away and practiced constantly — speaking with pebbles in his mouth to force clearer pronunciation, shouting speeches over the sound of the sea.

Whether every detail of that story happened exactly that way doesn't matter much. What matters is the order of operations: he trained alone, a lot, before he went back in front of people. He didn't wait until speaking stopped being scary. He made himself familiar enough with his own voice that the fear had less room to work.

That's what Solo Immersion is for. Every day, out loud, for ten to thirty minutes, you talk to yourself in the language you're acquiring — about your day, what you're going to do later, what you thought of something you watched. Not writing it, not reading it off a script. Saying it, out loud, to no one.

It feels ridiculous for about four days. Then it stops feeling ridiculous, and your own voice in the new language stops being a stranger's voice. That's the entire point. By the time a real person is on the other end of a conversation, at least one variable — the sound of yourself speaking this language — is no longer new.

## Lowering the stakes on purpose

You don't jump from silence to a job interview in the new language. You build a short staircase:

- Solo Immersion, alone, every day, until your own voice stops surprising you.
- A low-stakes real conversation, ten to fifteen minutes, with someone who already knows you're learning — through a language exchange app, a conversation club, or one paid lesson used only for talking, never for grammar.
- A slightly longer or slightly less comfortable one the week after that.
- A conversation where the topic actually matters to you, not one you rehearsed in advance.

Each step only asks you to tolerate a little more than the last one. That's the whole design. Nobody gets thrown into the deep end here.

Before any of those conversations, write down two or three rescue phrases in your notebook and have them ready without thinking: *could you repeat that, please?* *Could you speak a little slower?* *How do you say this?* *Sorry, I don't quite understand yet.*

Saying one of those out loud, calmly, does more for your confidence than almost anything else, because it proves that freezing isn't the only option when you get stuck. You have a move.

## What to expect the first few times

You will freeze. You'll ask someone to repeat something twice in the same sentence. You'll start a thought and abandon it halfway through because you ran out of words. None of that is a sign the method isn't working. It's what facing the fear actually looks like from the inside, every single time, for everyone.

What changes isn't the discomfort disappearing on day one. It's that the discomfort gets smaller, conversation by conversation, the same way it got smaller for me after my first real call in English with two strangers I'd never met before — a guy from the United States, a girl from Russia, on a free video platform I'd found digging through old forums. I mixed up words, asked them to repeat things, froze more than once. I also, for the first time, actually used the language to connect with somebody instead of just studying it. That difference is the whole game.

## FAQ

#### Why does avoiding a conversation make the fear worse instead of better?
Every skipped conversation feels like relief in the moment, and your brain logs that relief as proof that avoiding was the right call. The next time a similar moment comes up, the fear arrives a little faster and a little stronger, because it "worked" last time too. Facing a small, survivable version of the same situation teaches your brain the opposite lesson.

#### What is Solo Immersion, and why does it help with fear specifically?
Solo Immersion is talking out loud to yourself, alone, for ten to thirty minutes a day, in the language you're acquiring. It doesn't remove the fear directly — it makes your own voice in the new language familiar before a real person is ever involved, so one less thing feels foreign once the real conversation happens.

#### How do I work up to a real conversation without jumping in too fast?
Build a short staircase instead of a single leap: Solo Immersion alone first, then a low-stakes conversation with someone who already knows you're learning, then a slightly longer or less comfortable one, then a conversation on a topic that actually matters to you. Each step only asks for a little more than the last one.

#### What should I actually say when I freeze in the middle of a conversation?
Have two or three rescue phrases ready before you start: "Could you repeat that, please?", "Could you speak a little slower?", "How do you say this?", "Sorry, I don't quite understand yet." Saying one of these calmly turns a freeze into a normal exchange instead of a dead end.

## In short

Avoidance teaches your brain that avoiding was correct, and the fear grows to match it. Facing a smaller version of the same fear, on purpose, teaches it the opposite. Train your voice alone first, through Solo Immersion, until it stops sounding foreign to you. Then schedule a real conversation that's just slightly uncomfortable, not terrifying, and let the discomfort shrink the way it always does when you don't run from it.

I'll say this plainly, without dressing it up in a metaphor: I run the [Problem Solver Foundation](https://problemsolverfoundation.vercel.app/) because I believe the big problems — health, education, energy, security, economic stability — never get solved by one person working alone, and the goal, stated as directly as I can state it, is a billion people connected across professions and borders by 2035, solving things together instead of apart. Fear is one of the most ordinary reasons that never happens. Someone freezes, the call ends early, and a conversation that could have mattered never gets a second attempt. Losing the fear isn't just a personal win. It's one more conversation that gets to happen instead of not.

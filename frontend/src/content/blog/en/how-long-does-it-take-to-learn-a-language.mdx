Spanish took me about three months. French, three more after that. Italian, two more after that. I share those numbers because they happened to me — not because they're going to happen to you, and I want to explain exactly why before you read anything else into them.

## Why my numbers are almost useless to you

Spanish and Italian are close relatives of Portuguese, my native language. A huge amount of the vocabulary and sentence structure was already half-familiar before I started. On top of that, I had an entire community of polyglots around me, pushing me to practice, answering questions, giving me people to talk to on demand. Take away either of those two things — the language's distance from your own, or the density of real contact you get — and the timeline moves, a lot.

That's the honest answer to "how long does it take to learn a language," and it's not a satisfying one: it depends on the language you pick, how close it is to one you already speak, how much time you put in each day, how consistent you are, and how much real conversation you manage to get. Anyone who gives you one universal number — six weeks, six months, whatever — is selling you something, usually themselves.

## What I can actually promise you instead

The method I built, which I call the Language Acquisition Formula, is organized around a 12-week roadmap. I'm careful about how I describe that number, because it matters: it's a path, not a guarantee. It's not a promise that you'll be fluent on day 84. It's a promise that on every one of those 84 days, you'll know exactly what to do and why, instead of guessing, which is the actual thing that makes people quit.

The roadmap breaks into six stages, each one checked off by a concrete task instead of a date on the calendar:

**Stage 0 — Planning.** One day. You write down your reason, a goal you can actually observe yourself hitting, and a fixed daily time slot.

**Stage 1 — Alphabet and sounds.** A few hours to a few days, depending on whether the language shares your alphabet.

**Stage 2 — Vocabulary.** About a week, working through the roughly 200 words that carry most of everyday conversation.

**Stage 3 — Listening and reading.** About a week, built around seven short stories made almost entirely from those same 200 words.

**Stage 4 — Creating need.** Weeks 3 through 8, where your actual life becomes the study material.

**Stage 5 — Losing the fear.** Weeks 9 through 12, ending in a real, unscripted conversation.

Someone with two hours a day moves through this faster than someone with thirty minutes. Someone learning a language close to their own moves faster than someone starting a language with a different alphabet and no shared vocabulary at all. The checkpoint always decides when you move on — never the week number.

## Why cramming makes the whole thing feel slower than it is

If you've ever crammed for a test and forgotten almost everything a month later, you already understand the actual science here, even if you've never heard the name attached to it. In the 1880s, a psychologist named Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tracked exactly how fast he forgot them. Most of the loss happened in the first hours and the first day. After that, forgetting slowed down a lot. More recent studies have reproduced almost the exact same curve.

What that means practically: five hours of study on a single excited Saturday, followed by two weeks of nothing, loses most of its value before the next session even starts. Research on distributed practice backs this up directly — the same total number of hours pays off far more when it's spread into short, frequent sessions than when it's packed into a few long ones. Thirty minutes a day beats three and a half hours once a week, for the exact same total time invested, because the reviews land closer to the point where you'd otherwise forget.

This is also why the timeline question is a bit of a trap. People ask "how long will this take" expecting the answer to be about talent or difficulty, when it's usually about rhythm. The person who shows up every day for twelve weeks, in thirty-minute pieces, ends up faster than the person who throws a weekend at it twice a month — not because they're smarter, but because memory simply doesn't work the second way.

## What actually moves the number

If you want to know what to control instead of what to worry about, it's this: how close the language is to one you already speak, how many minutes a day you give it, how consistent those minutes are, and how much real conversation you get — not video calls with a translator standing by, actual unscripted exchanges with someone who answers back. I walk through exactly how to build each of those, stage by stage, with the daily tasks laid out, in [the full roadmap](/en/blog/the-language-acquisition-formula).

The honest version of my own story is this: it wasn't that Spanish was "easy" and some other language would be "hard." It's that I had three of the four variables already stacked in my favor — language distance, daily contact, and real conversation on demand — and I still needed to actually show up every day to collect on them. The fourth variable, consistency, is the one no language and no community can hand you. That one's always yours.

## Stop asking for a number, start asking for a plan

If you take one thing from this, take this: trade the question "how long will this take me" for "what am I doing today, and does it match what I did yesterday." A plan you can actually check off beats a deadline you're guessing at, every time.
